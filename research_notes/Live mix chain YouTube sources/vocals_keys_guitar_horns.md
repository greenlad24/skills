## Lead vocal chain (warm, intimate, upfront), male vs female, and backing vocals

### Takeaway
The verified consensus starting chain:
- **HPF:** about 100 Hz (higher for female voices and live stages; 120–180 Hz in the most conservative source).
- **Cuts:** a wide cut at 200–300 Hz for mud, a cut around 500–600 Hz for box or honk, and a narrow look at 2–4 kHz for harshness.
- **Air:** a high-shelf lift from 6 to 10 kHz.
- **Sibilance:** 5–8 kHz, handled with a de-esser.
- **Compression:** 2:1–4:1, attack 10–15 ms, release 50–250 ms, about 3–6 dB of gain reduction (GR). One high-view source goes to 10–12 dB when attack and release are set well.
- **Two-stage compression** shows up in an R&B studio chain (channel compressors plus vocal-bus compressor) and in Attaway's analog-plus-digital approach, but nobody gives numbers for it.

Male vs female differs mainly in where "body" sits: about 100 Hz on low male voices and about 200 Hz on female and high male voices. Several sources flag 4 kHz as the female harshness spot.

### Cited Findings
**HPF**
- HPF about 100 Hz is "a fairly safe setting" unless the singer is a deep baritone. Raise it until it starts thinning the voice, then back off. It also reduces plosives. [verified: 403k] — [Alan Hamilton Audio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oP4sdpkkNhY)
- A contrasting, more aggressive live rule: "put a low cut filter on every single vocal somewhere between 120 and 180 hertz." [verified: 809k] — [Collaborate Worship](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nF0uezOA4UA). This conflicts with the 100 Hz guidance above. For a broadcast mix with no stage-wash concern, the 100 Hz end is the gentler option.
- 100 Hz is "the lowest end of warmth"; "I would usually high pass everything below 100 hertz." [verified: 1.01M] — [Colt Capperrune](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Wq1di2luMcs)
- Don't boost 80 Hz to fatten a voice; boost about 160 Hz by a few dB instead. [verified: 403k] — [Alan Hamilton Audio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oP4sdpkkNhY)

**Corrective EQ**
- Too thick: cut about 250 Hz. Honky: cut about 600 Hz. Harsh: cut in the high mids. Make only one or two cuts, a few dB each, and judge them with the band playing, not soloed. [verified: 403k] — [Alan Hamilton Audio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oP4sdpkkNhY)
- Attaway's live vocal EQ:
  - a wide cut around 200–250 Hz;
  - a cut around 500 Hz (±100 Hz), noting that too much loses body;
  - a narrow cut around 2 kHz (±an octave).
  - His map of the high mids: 1 kHz is nasal, 2 kHz sits between nasal and piercing, 4 kHz is piercing. [verified: 444k] — [Attaway Audio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ey715RqVozk)
- Colt Capperrune's frequency map: 200 Hz is warmth; 300–500 Hz is boxy/honky/room ("I will almost never boost this"); about 2 kHz is presence that makes the vocal "pop out"; 4 kHz is "where the harshness... the piercing quality" lives, though too little 4 kHz hurts intelligibility; 6 kHz is clarity; and cutting 4k with 6k thickens a thin or harsh voice. [verified: 1.01M] — [Colt Capperrune](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Wq1di2luMcs)
- How to find a problem frequency: set a peaking band to +9 dB at Q 4, sweep while the singer sings, then cut at the offending frequency. [verified: 809k] — [Collaborate Worship](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nF0uezOA4UA)

**Air and sibilance**
- About 10 kHz lift for "air"; a high-mid lift helps the vocal cut through a guitar-heavy mix. Sibilance is "generally a 5k to 8k problem," handled by a small cut or a de-esser. [verified: 403k] — [Alan Hamilton Audio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oP4sdpkkNhY)

**Male vs female**
- "Higher end male voices and most female voices don't have a lot of 100 hertz... 200 on a female or a high male voice is... where the warmth and where the body lies." [verified: 1.01M] — [Colt Capperrune](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Wq1di2luMcs)
- Female vocal worked example:
  - mud cut about 4.5 dB at 300 Hz (range 250–300);
  - boxiness cut 3 dB at about 600 Hz;
  - a high shelf from about 6 kHz;
  - about 4 kHz is "on especially female vocals... a really common spot to have issues with harshness";
  - a resonance just below 800 Hz.
  - Tagged [1 video, 57.6k], but corroborated: 250–300 Hz mud (Alan Hamilton 403k, Attaway 444k), 600 Hz honk (Alan Hamilton 403k), 4 kHz harsh (Colt 1.01M) → [4 channels agree on these frequencies]. — [Worship Sound Guy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NeqfK1Z9JKI)

**Compression**
- Ratio 2:1–3:1 is "fairly safe," up to 4:1 for an inconsistent singer. Use auto attack/release where the console offers it. Aim for the top 2–3 GR lights flickering, which smooths peaks only. Live needs a lighter touch than studio. [verified: 403k] — [Alan Hamilton Audio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oP4sdpkkNhY)
- Collaborate Worship's starting compressor: ratio 3, attack about 10 ms, release about 50 ms, makeup +6 dB, threshold set so GR reads about −6 dB at the loudest moments. "If you compress more than 6 dB, the vocal starts to sound lifeless and smashed." [verified: 809k] — [Collaborate Worship](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nF0uezOA4UA)
- Attaway's starting compressor and attack/release reasoning:
  - Settings: 4:1, soft knee, attack 12–15 ms, release about 250 ms. Release faster for fast lyrics.
  - Attack shapes tone: a slower attack sounds thicker and a faster attack thinner. When a singer gets harsh going loud and high, slow the attack rather than speeding it up.
  - Detector mode: RMS can run faster than peak mode and sounds more open.
  - Amount: "if you get the attack and the release time set right I can go to 10 or 12 dB of compression." He contradicts the 6 dB ceiling above.
  - He pairs a lighter analog stage with a digital compressor, an implicit two-stage approach. [verified: 258k] — [Attaway Audio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vcdwTwAXjmg)

**R&B studio chain (Adam Lewis Mixing)**
- Channel stage, in order:
  1. Logic EQ with HPF and a slight boost at 200 Hz.
  2. SSL channel with a high-end boost and a low-mid dip.
  3. R-Vox, light leveling.
  4. De-esser.
  5. Main compressor, which "push[es] down the peaks and bring[s] the dips up."
- Vocal-bus stage, in order:
  1. Light compressor.
  2. De-esser.
  3. CLA Vocals (EQ plus a little reverb and delay).
  4. De-esser on partial mix.
  5. Dynamic EQ that boosts the high mids/highs and low mids but compresses those bands when they get loud.
- Sends: a stereo ping-pong delay, an exciter on an "air bus," and a main reverb.
- No numeric values given. [verified: 125k] — [Adam Lewis Mixing](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MTmteq-jCyw)

**Backing vocals**
- Tony Maserati treats background vocals as a blend: he compresses the BV "package" so it is "up in your face" as one unit, and avoids individual doctoring. [verified: 646k] — [Mix with the Masters](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a6agrpAFmGw)
- BV presence lives at 800 Hz–4 kHz. Make a wide cut (Q about 2) there, or use a high shelf to roll off highs, so the lead stands out. Put BVs in a subgroup and EQ the group. [1 video, 22k], partly corroborated: group processing also comes from Maserati 646k → the "group/bus the BVs" idea is [2 channels]; the specific 800 Hz–4 kHz cut stays **unverified**. — [Collaborate Worship](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H7tyAlk6Zd4)

### Inferences
- For Sunrise Jam, a lead-vocal starting point that stays inside the verified envelope:
  - HPF 90–100 Hz (male) or about 120 Hz (female);
  - −2 to −4 dB wide at 250–300 Hz;
  - −2 to −3 dB at 500–600 Hz only if boxy;
  - a narrow dip at 2–4 kHz only if harsh (female most often near 4 kHz);
  - +2 to 3 dB high shelf at 8–10 kHz;
  - de-esser at 5–8 kHz;
  - comp 1 (leveler): 3:1, about 10–15 ms attack, about 50–250 ms release, 3–6 dB GR;
  - an optional light second stage, as on the R&B bus. Its numbers would be extrapolated, since no source gave them.
- The "warm, intimate" neo-soul character points to a slower attack (Attaway: thicker) and keeping 200 Hz rather than cutting it hard (Colt: 200 Hz is warmth).
- For backing vocals: a higher HPF, some high-mid or presence reduction, and bus compression, so they blend under the lead.

### Gaps
- No YouTube source gave numeric two-stage settings (e.g., an 1176-style fast peak stage followed by an LA-2A-style leveler with specific GR per stage).
- No neo-soul/funk-specific vocal settings were found. The Russell Elevado interview ([RBMA, 64k](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a3qg1-VZUik)) has no numbers.
- De-esser threshold/range numbers were not stated. Daniel Kharrat's X32 de-essing videos (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fcdY5nQxPn4, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kKUKktGe3jQ) were found in search but not transcribed, and their view counts are unknown.

## Keys: Rhodes, piano and pads (stereo DI); sitting "behind the singer"; width and mono

### Takeaway
Two channels agree on the keys frequency map:
- mud at 400–600 Hz, or 200 Hz boom on piano;
- 1–2 kHz decides whether the instrument is foreground or background;
- 3–5 kHz is presence and brightness.

To sit keys behind the vocal, avoid boosting (or slightly cut) 1–2 kHz and keep presence modest. Keys compression should be light: keep the transient with a slower attack, and use a lower ratio with slow attack and release on pads. Pensado's Rhodes treatment is "barely moving" compression plus saturation, chorus/Dimension D and reverb for a silky bed. No source covered mono compatibility numerically.

### Cited Findings
- Keys and synths "get a bit muddy in the 400 to 600 Hertz range": use a peaking cut at Q about 4. Boost 1–2 kHz to cut through, "or you may even need to cut a little in this area to make room for other instruments." 3–4 kHz is presence and clarity: boost for clarity, cut to darken. Piano: low shelf about 200 Hz, cut 3–6 dB. Brighten with a 3 kHz peak at Q about 4 (too much exaggerates damper noise). [93k; partially corroborated below] — [Collaborate Worship](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XJaEkLbxoEk)
- More 1–2 kHz on piano "is literally like the difference between the piano being a background instrument and being like a priority instrument." The 5 kHz shelf adds presence ("closer to you"), while 10–12 kHz adds air without presence. 100–200 Hz gets a boost or cut depending on the mix. [64k] — [Hardcore Music Studio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fM9OwnQ_uRI). The 1–2 kHz role is [2 channels agree, 93k + 64k → verified].
- Piano compression has three goals: dynamic range, attack and sustain. Use a slower attack to let the hammer transient through (peak mode). Use a faster attack, with release timed to the music, to enhance sustain. Pads: lower ratio, slower attack and slow release to fatten. Many pad patches are mixed too bright, so a high shelf cut may be needed. [83k; consistent with the light-touch advice from Collaborate Worship 93k → 2 channels on "keys need little EQ/light comp"] — [Attaway Audio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UXF70zgc-Q4)
- Dave Pensado on a Jill Scott Rhodes:
  - compressed "a little bit... the meter didn't move";
  - EQ "getting rid of a little bit of the mud";
  - Eventide pitch shift for an "ethereal" vibe;
  - multiband saturation;
  - reverb, plus a "vitalizer getting a little bit of width";
  - "the old classic Dimension D."
  - The goal was "a bed of... silky and sweet" behind her voice. [99.5k, single source; the numbers are absent, so the approach is noted as expert opinion and is close to the 100k threshold] — [Pensado's Place](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FmdQsxbR5Oc)

### Inferences
- "Low mids behind the singer" in practice:
  - Keep Rhodes body around 200–400 Hz, minus the 400–600 Hz mud.
  - Make a gentle −1 to −3 dB wide dip at about 1–3 kHz, where the vocal presence (2–4 kHz) lives.
  - Don't boost 5 kHz.
  - Keep comp to 1–3 dB.
- Chorus/Dimension-style width should be checked in mono, because chorus-based width can comb-filter when summed. That warning comes from general knowledge, not from these videos.

### Gaps
- No YouTube source gave numeric stereo-width or mono-compatibility guidance for stereo keyboard DIs (e.g., pan L/R vs 60%, or a mid/side check).
- No source gave a numeric Rhodes compressor ratio or attack.

## Electric guitar (funk clean rhythm and lead; amp mic and modeler/DI)

### Takeaway
Verified across 2 channels (178k + 526k):
- HPF about 90–100 Hz (clean guitars can go to about 150–200 Hz);
- a small dip around 4 kHz for harshness.

Hardcore Music Studio (178k) adds the rest of the frequency map:
- 1 kHz puts the guitar up front;
- 2–2.5 kHz gives bite for leads;
- 5 kHz is pick attack and brightness;
- 8 kHz is airy brightness.

Cory Wong's own clean funk sound is a DI/modeler chain: an API 2500 at about 50% wet/dry (parallel-style) ahead of a clean amp sim that doesn't break up, with HPF 90 Hz and a slight 4 kHz dip.

### Cited Findings
- HPF most electric guitars "around 100 Hertz give or take about 20"; on clean guitar "we can high pass filter all the way up to 200."
- 1 kHz "put[s] your guitars up front"; 2–2.5 kHz is "edgy bitey aggressive" for "an extra layer or lead guitar that I really wanted to cut through."
- 5 kHz gives brightness plus "scratch and pick attack"; 8 kHz is fizzy/airy.
- Harshness: notch "right around 4K," don't nuke it.
- Warning: boosting 1k/2.5k/5k on many guitars makes the mix thin and harsh. [verified: 178k] — [Hardcore Music Studio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xYK-GM3MPD8)
- Cory Wong (Vulfpeck), DI-recorded:
  - API 2500 compressor with mix at about 50% ("too much... starts to feeling choked");
  - SSL channel with HPF ("other producers like to do 150 on my guitar, I like to do 90"), "dip a little bit around 4k," and a tiny top-end roll-off;
  - "I love it when amps don't break up at all."
  - Often he runs the compressor into an Ampeg SVT model, mids about 3 o'clock and bass about 9 o'clock, mirroring his Wampler Ego pedal used at about 50% blend.
  - [verified: 526k] — [Universal Audio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z3fStcglxoQ)
- Cross-check: the 4 kHz dip and the 90–100 Hz HPF appear in both videos → [2 channels, 178k + 526k].

### Inferences
- Funk rhythm (scratch) guitar starting point:
  - HPF 100–150 Hz;
  - −2 dB at about 4 kHz;
  - a modest 5 kHz lift only if the scratch disappears;
  - compression via parallel or 50% mix, so transients stay.
- Lead guitar: add 1–2.5 kHz rather than more 5 kHz.
- A DI/modeler feed needs the same EQ as a mic'd amp, plus a low-pass around 8–10 kHz if the modeler's cab sim is fizzy. That low-pass value is extrapolated: no source gave a numeric LPF.

### Gaps
- No funk-specific amp-mic placement or compressor attack/release numbers were found on YouTube. Cory Wong's Top Funk Guitar Tips (MusicRadar, 242k, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=A01w3Bhsz9A) was found but not transcribed.

## Acoustic guitar DI

### Takeaway
Two 100k+ channels agree:
- HPF about 100 Hz or a little above;
- a low-mid cut (about 250–315 Hz);
- a resonance cut around 630 Hz–1.2 kHz (900 Hz–1 kHz is "normally the problem");
- a careful sparkle lift at 10–12 kHz;
- fast-attack, soft-knee, medium-release compression;
- little or no reverb live.

### Cited Findings
- Attaway Audio:
  - Roll the HPF above the 82 Hz low E.
  - "A lot of guitars will resonate around 315 and 630 Hertz," and an octave up at 1.2 kHz. Small cuts at the octaves clean it up.
  - "Too much 10k is gonna make the guitar sound machine."
  - Compression: soft knee, faster attack to tame the transient, medium release (faster for picking, slower for strumming).
  - "In a live setting I rarely add reverb." [verified: 106k] — [Attaway Audio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H6_L4X_ftNM)
- OwnTheMix: HPF 100 Hz; sweep to find a problem at about 900 Hz and a low-mid at about 250 Hz and cut both; add 12 kHz for sparkle. "Normally the problem on an acoustic guitar is in your mid-range somewhere around 900 Hertz or 1K." [verified: 106k] — [OwnTheMix](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=enc6oUhyT3c)
- Unverified (2.2k views, single channel): HPF starting about 147 Hz; cuts around 500 Hz and 3 kHz; high cut from about 8–10 kHz; aggressive 10:1 fast compressor; reverb decay 1.5–2.5 s; −18 dBFS gain-staging reference. — [Chris Green](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EBigNgXpE4)

### Inferences
- Acoustic DI starting point:
  - HPF 100–120 Hz;
  - −2 to −3 dB at 250 Hz;
  - −2 to −4 dB narrow somewhere at 600 Hz–1.2 kHz (find the quack/resonance);
  - +1 to 2 dB shelf at 10–12 kHz;
  - comp 3:1–4:1, fast attack, medium release.
- Ratio and the ms values are inferred. No 100k+ source gave them.

### Gaps
- No high-view source gave numeric acoustic-DI compressor ratio, attack or release values.

## Sax and flute (wireless clip mic), trombone, and harmonica

### Takeaway
Agreement across channels:
- HPF horns at about 100 Hz (sax and trombone, 3 channels), with flute and sax HPF up to 190–200 Hz in a dense mix (2 channels).
- Harshness around 4 kHz on sax (and voices).
- A "fizzy/airy" band around 2–2.2 kHz on sax and trombone (2 channels).
- Mud at about 350–600 Hz (sax, trombone, flute).
- A high-shelf or air lift at 7–12 kHz.
- Brass treated "the same way I would approach a vocal," with fast-attack compression and a medium release.

Every horn source is under 100k views. Findings stand only where 2+ channels agree. Harmonica has **no** engineer-level numeric source on YouTube; the only advice is amp-tone guidance from harp players (turn the treble down; higher-key harps darker).

### Cited Findings
**Saxophone**
- Recording Crave: alto/tenor sax compressed hard, about 9–10 dB GR ("in the track this works really well").
  - EQ: about +3 dB around 7 kHz, HPF from 100 Hz.
  - Second sax: +2.5 dB at 12 kHz, −3.5 dB at 2 kHz with "fairly generous bandwidth."
  - Reverb pre-delay 38 ms. [37.9k] — [Recording Crave](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jPe8O7LWitg)
- Fabio Marziali:
  - HPF 200 Hz, "push... around 3000 Hz" for punch;
  - comp ratio about 2:1 with about 5 dB GR;
  - a short ambience reverb (about 0.5 s) plus a main reverb (about 1.x s);
  - echo synced to 1/8 note, feedback 33%, wet 2%. [22k] — [Fabio Marziali](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=33aHekBUbHc)
- The Home Recording Network on alto sax: high shelf from about 8 kHz, boost somewhere at 1–3 kHz, and "around 4k it's pretty typical to find some harshness with things like saxophone even voices." [23.3k] — [The Home Recording Network](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6jhM8zHVRAI)
- Corroboration tallies for sax:
  - HPF 100–200 Hz: 3 channels.
  - High-shelf or air lift 7–12 kHz: 2 channels (Recording Crave, HRN).
  - 4 kHz harshness: HRN plus the vocal sources Colt (1.01M) and Hardcore guitar (178k) put harshness at 4 kHz generally.
  - **Conflict:** 2 kHz cut (Recording Crave) vs 3 kHz boost (Marziali).
  - Compression amount ranges 5 dB (Marziali) to 10 dB (Recording Crave).
- Recording Crave on horns: a sax EQ with a notch plus a small boost around 160 Hz; each brass instrument EQ'd, then grouped to one bus. [66.7k] — [Recording Crave](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eDyiBZ69HUw)
- Better Sax tested wireless clip-ons and played them dry: no EQ or effects, only gain adjusted. Clip-ons sound noticeably different from a ribbon reference. No settings given. [verified view count 133k, but contains no settings] — [Better Sax](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NGve-bFBfF4)

**Brass / trombone**
- Derek "MixedByAli" Ali: approach brass "the same exact way I would approach a vocal... present... lush not harsh." He uses an 1176 with fast attack (7 on the 1176 dial) and medium release, sometimes all-buttons for grit. [59k] — [Universal Audio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3OOwjTQABVc)
- Shawn Bell's trombone chain:
  - EQ: HPF 80–100 Hz with a gentle slope; cut tubbiness/room at 400–600 Hz, with a smaller cut at about 200 Hz; a narrow, deeper cut at about 2.2 kHz (fizzy, airy band 2–3.5 kHz); boost about 10 kHz after the compressor.
  - Compressor: 2:1–3:1 (starts at 3:1), attack 10 ms, release 120 ms.
  - [4.2k, low view] — [Shawn Bell](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zpBd3MBeIFM)
- How the trombone findings are corroborated:
  - The HPF of about 100 Hz matches the sax sources → [3 channels].

**Flute (unverified; single channel, 15.9k)**

**Harmonica (unverified; no engineer source found)**
- Harp players' amp advice: harmonica is "a very bright trebley instrument." Turn the amp tone control down (warmer) and keep gain under halfway, because high gain gets "really harsh" and feeds back. Higher-key harps (e.g., D) should be darker, lower keys (A, G) can be a bit brighter. [Tomlin Leckie, 33.7k and 30.8k, same channel, so 1 channel] — [Amplified Harmonica Quickstart](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pV8wgssIUdI), [Ultimate Blues Harmonica Set Up](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XV5xxd0RoHY)

- Web supplement (forum opinion, not YouTube): boost 200–500 Hz and gently reduce treble in the 2–5 kHz range; the ideal EQ depends on the harp's key. — [Modern Blues Harmonica forum](https://www.modernbluesharmonica.com/board/board_topic/5560960/5496850.htm). General mix advice: "cut between 2 kHz and 3 kHz to smooth a harsh sounding" part — [Scribd Mix Tips](https://www.scribd.com/document/63845321/Mix-Tips).

### Inferences
- Sax/flute on a wireless clip mic, starting point:
  - HPF 120–200 Hz (clip mics pick up key noise and body thump);
  - −2 to −4 dB at 350–500 Hz;
  - sweep 2–4 kHz and cut the worst spot 2–4 dB, narrow;
  - +2 dB shelf at 8–12 kHz;
  - comp 2:1–3:1, about 10 ms attack, about 100–150 ms release, 3–6 dB GR.
  - The ratio and timing borrow the trombone and vocal values; they are not horn-verified.
- Harmonica: treat it like a harsh vocal.
  - HPF about 150 Hz;
  - keep or gently lift 200–500 Hz for warmth;
  - sweep 2–5 kHz (start near 3–4 kHz) and cut 3–6 dB;
  - use a dynamic EQ or multiband band on that region so the cut engages only on loud bends, as on Adam Lewis's dynamic-EQ approach to vocals;
  - optionally low-pass at about 8–10 kHz.
  - All of this is inference from the vocal and harshness sources plus the forum advice, **not** a verified harmonica recipe.

## Reverb and delay for vocals and horns (soul/funk)

### Takeaway
Verified live-vocal effects starting points:
- **Reverb:** about 1.5 s decay as a baseline (shorter for fast songs, longer for ballads); hall or plate (plate is brighter); pre-delay in the tens of ms up to 100–150 ms to keep the vocal in front; HPF and LPF on the reverb return, optionally cutting 2 kHz on the return; return trimmed −12 dB to get fader resolution.
- **Delay:** tempo-synced 1/4 or 1/8 notes, feedback about 35% (1/4) or up to about 40% (1/8), HPF on the delay up to about 400 Hz; 20–50 ms for doubling, 50–100 ms for slap.
- **Ducking:** sidechain the reverb and delay to the vocal for a clean but lush sound: 7–8:1, 5–8 dB GR, fast attack, longer release.

### Cited Findings
**Reverb**
- About 1.5 s is a baseline vocal reverb decay, shorter for faster songs, longer for ballads. Mix effects to enhance, not overwhelm. [verified: 403k] — [Alan Hamilton Audio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oP4sdpkkNhY). Consistent with Chris Green's 1.5–2.5 s (2.2k) → [2 channels].
- Attaway Audio on vocal reverb:
  - Hall works well; plate is "fun" but "a little bit bright right out of the box."
  - Start the reverb return trimmed −12 dB so its fader sits near 0.
  - Pre-delay adds separation; he demonstrates 100 ms and 150 ms to push the vocal "way out in front."
  - Filter the reverb send/return (HPF and LPF) and optionally cut some 2 kHz to declutter. [verified: 267k] — [Attaway Audio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ms0qDW0GSDc)
- Sax reverb pre-delay 38 ms [37.9k] — [Recording Crave](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jPe8O7LWitg). Sax: a short ambience reverb of about 0.5 s plus a longer main reverb [22k] — [Fabio Marziali](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=33aHekBUbHc). Horn reverb therefore = [2 low-view channels]: a short plus medium reverb, with pre-delay in the tens of ms.

**Delay**
- Attaway Audio on vocal delay:
  - Tempo-synced 1/4 and 1/8 notes. With 1/8 notes, "I can kick my feedback up to about 40%"; with 1/4 notes, about 35%. Tap differently per section (1/8 on verse, 1/4 on chorus).
  - Put an HPF on the delay, "even roll it up to about 400 Hertz," for a thin delay that doesn't clutter.
  - For space: 20–100 ms with a little feedback, filtered. 20–50 ms feels like a doubler or small room; 50–100 ms is slap. [verified: 107k] — [Attaway Audio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2t_hVmyXZ-4)
- 1/8-note echo on sax at 33% feedback corroborates the tempo-synced 1/8 at about 35–40% feedback → [2 channels, 107k + 22k] — [Fabio Marziali](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=33aHekBUbHc). The flute video also uses a quarter-note delay — [The Home Recording Network](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iB1pzQlxqJs).
- A stereo ping-pong delay on an R&B lead vocal fills the gaps between phrases. [verified: 125k] — [Adam Lewis Mixing](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MTmteq-jCyw)

**Ducking**
- Sidechain the reverb and delay returns to the lead vocal: ratio about 7–8:1, threshold for −5 to −8 dB GR, 100% mix, no makeup, fairly quick attack, longer release, so the effects bloom between phrases. [verified: 1.25M] — [Streaky](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CUyMUHStPxs)

### Inferences
- Neo-soul broadcast vocal FX set:
  - Plate or hall at about 1.6–2.2 s with 30–80 ms pre-delay, HPF about 200–300 Hz and LPF about 6–8 kHz on the return (the filter values are my extrapolation; Attaway gives no numbers).
  - 1/8 or dotted-1/8 tempo delay at about 30–35% feedback with HPF about 400 Hz.
  - Both returns ducked about 5 dB by the vocal, as Streaky does.
- Horns can share the vocal plate at a lower send.
- Because the broadcast mix runs in the cloud with no feedback risk, boosts on the effect returns are safe.

- No funk- or soul-specific reverb type or decay was found from a named engineer (e.g., an Elevado or Pensado numeric setting).
