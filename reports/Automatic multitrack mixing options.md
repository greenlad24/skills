# Fit our own console, use RoEx as the second opinion

No open model you can download can take our 16 raw MR18 channels plus a Scary Pockets reference and hand back per-channel settings we can legally load into the live LV2 engine. The model built for exactly that job, **Diff-MST**, has no public weights and a non-commercial licence. So the best "teacher" for the setup phase is not a model at all. It is **our own parameter fit, run on our own chain**. We render the recorded multitracks offline through the same mod-host/LV2 plugins we use live. Then we search their parameters (gain, pan, EQ bands, compressor, reverb send, master) with Apache-2.0 tools (GRAFX or dasp-pytorch as fast stand-ins, and CMA-ES through the real plugins) until the mix's measured features match Mix F's balance and the reference's tone. This method is published (ST-ITO, Diff-MST's design). It gives numbers we can copy one-to-one into `param_set`, costs nothing beyond compute, and keeps us inside the licences. **RoEx Tonn's settings-only API** is the best paid "second opinion". It takes 2–32 stems at 48 kHz and returns gain, pan, six EQ bands and a compressor per track for about **$1–1.25 per song**. It has no reference-track input, though, and its quality claims come only from the vendor. **StemFX** (MIT code and weights, reference-based, but only 4 separated stems) and **automix-toolkit DMC** (Apache-2.0, gain and pan only) are cheap extra baselines. Judging results needs the most care. Audio-LLM judges have a documented position bias, which is exactly why Gemini said "Y, high confidence" on identical clips. Decisions should therefore rest on loudness-matched objective distances, include a built-in "identical clips must tie" check, and end with the user's own blind listening.

## The setup-phase job is narrower than "automatic mixing"

The wider literature solves a harder problem than ours: making a good mix from nothing. We already have an approved target, **Mix F**: lean-low, bright, dense, vocal sitting about +2 dB in the band, supporting channels about −4 dB, drums prominent, round kick. F already exists as parameters in our own chain. So the teacher has two jobs. First, carry F's *measured character* onto new recordings where the players and instruments change. Second, pull the tone and balance toward the Scary Pockets reference without breaking F. Whatever the tool, the output has to become LSP/x42/Dragonfly/Calf parameter values. That rules out anything that only returns audio, and it makes "interpretable parameters" the main ranking test, ahead of headline quality.

Three facts about our material affect every option. The MR18 tap is **48 kHz**, while most research models expect 44.1 kHz. Diff-MST resamples internally, and so does Matchering ([eval_all_combo.py](https://github.com/sai-soum/Diff-MST/blob/main/scripts/eval_all_combo.py), [Matchering defaults](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sergree/matchering/master/matchering/defaults.py)). Our recordings are **live, with stage bleed**. No automix system found in the notes was evaluated on live multitracks or on funk/soul ([MEGAMI arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.08040), [Diff-MST arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08889v1)). And show recordings are archived as **MP3**, but RoEx's API refuses MP3 ([Tonn portal](https://tonn-portal.roexaudio.com/)). For setup-phase test recordings we should keep the WAVs.

### Building the fit ourselves wins on fidelity and licence

The fit ranks first because it is the only route that is reference-aware, outputs our exact parameters, and is commercial-safe. GRAFX (Apache-2.0, `pip install grafx`, pushed June 2026) builds PyTorch graphs of differentiable processors, and its showcase optimises every parameter of a full mixing graph by gradient descent ([GRAFX](https://github.com/sh-lee97/grafx), [arXiv 2408.03204](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.03204)). dasp-pytorch (Apache-2.0) provides differentiable gain, parametric EQ, compressor, panner and a noise-shaped reverb, and Diff-MST itself depends on it ([dasp-pytorch](https://github.com/csteinmetz1/dasp-pytorch)). A proxy is still not our plugin, though. The dasp compressor has a different detector and knee from LSP's, and no source measures the gap. So the final pass should be gradient-free search through the real plugins. **ST-ITO** proved this pattern on Linux: CMA-ES (population 64, at most 25 steps) over 63 open-source VST3 plugins, about **1 minute per example**, rated at least on par with DeepAFx-ST+ by 23 listeners ([ST-ITO](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21233v1)). One tooling gap matters. **Neither DawDreamer nor Pedalboard hosts LV2**, only VST3 and (in DawDreamer) Faust ([DawDreamer](https://github.com/DBraun/DawDreamer), [Pedalboard](https://github.com/spotify/pedalboard)). LSP ships VST3 builds, but x42 and Calf are, to our knowledge, LV2-only. The most faithful renderer is therefore our own mod-host graph on JACK's dummy backend in freewheel mode, with `lv2apply` for fast single-plugin renders of one channel ([lv2apply](https://manpages.ubuntu.com/manpages/noble/man1/lv2apply.1.html)). For the master tone, Matchering (GPL-3.0) matches RMS, frequency response, peak level and stereo width to a reference ([Matchering](https://github.com/sergree/matchering)). We should use only its smoothed correction curve, fitted to LSP EQ bands. Its output must never feed the stream: it targets near-0 dBFS sample peak and the reference's RMS, not −16 LUFS and our true-peak ceiling ([defaults.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sergree/matchering/master/matchering/defaults.py)).

### RoEx is the only real paid API, and it cannot hear a reference

RoEx Tonn is the only verified, self-serve developer API that takes multitracks and returns settings. A `/mixpreview` request carries per-track `instrumentGroup`, `presenceSetting`, `panPreference` and `reverbPreference`, plus `musicalStyle` and `sampleRate` 48000. `/retrievefinalmix` accepts edited per-track settings and re-renders, and `retrieveFXSettings` returns gain, pan angle (−60…+60), six EQ bands (±12 dB, Q 0.1–10) and a compressor ([OpenAPI spec](https://tonn-portal.roexaudio.com/openapi.json)). That maps cleanly onto LSP `para_equalizer` and compressor parameters. Two caveats limit it. First, there is **no reference-track parameter** for mixing, and reference-matched mastering is still "Coming Soon" ([Getting started](https://tonn-portal.roexaudio.com/docs/getting-started)). Second, RoEx's mix includes reverb decisions, but the settings schema we saw has no reverb field. Pricing is fair for setup work: credit packs of $10 for 1,000 credits up to $80 for 10,000, valid 12 months, at 125 credits per settings-only mix ([RoEx pricing](https://tonn-portal.roexaudio.com/pricing)). A $10 pack covers about **eight settings-only songs**. The terms let RoEx "host, share, listen to, and modify" our content to improve its services, and they forbid us from using the service to "train artificial intelligence" ([API Terms](https://tonn-portal.roexaudio.com/api-terms)). That clause needs care: copying RoEx's settings into a learned playbook could be read as training. **Ask RoEx before automating that step.** Music.AI resells the RoEx mixer at $1.20 per minute, roughly five times the direct price for a 5-minute song, and its page does not make clear whether that module takes stems ([Music.AI pricing](https://music.ai/pricing/)). Its $0.02/min "Reference mastering" is a cheap way to cross-check our master tone. LANDR, Masterchannel and Cryo Mix's white-label product are stereo mastering only, and Dolby's mastering API now redirects to its streaming platform ([Cryo Mix comparison, competitor source](https://cryo-mix.com/blog/posts/ai-mixing-and-mastering-in-2026-what-each-platform-actually-does-and-what-it-doesnt); [Dolby redirect](https://docs.dolby.io/media/docs/music-mastering-api-guide)).

### The research models are useful only as baselines or as blueprints

Diff-MST has exactly our design: raw tracks plus a reference song in, per-track gain, pan, EQ and compressor plus a master EQ and compressor out. It was trained with up to 16 tracks, and "performance declined beyond the trained track count" ([arXiv 2407.08889](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08889v1)). But the checkpoint was never released. Issue #27 has asked for it since September 2024, and the inference script hard-codes the author's local path ([issue #27](https://github.com/sai-soum/Diff-MST/issues/27)). The code is CC BY-NC-SA ([LICENSE](https://github.com/sai-soum/Diff-MST/blob/main/LICENSE)). Settings learned from it would flow into a monetised stream, so treat it as a blueprint and do not depend on it. Asking the authors for weights for private comparison costs nothing. StemFX is the only recent reference-based model with **MIT code and MIT weights** ([StemFX](https://github.com/barry-mir/stemfx), [HF](https://huggingface.co/barry-mir/stemfx-bsfilm)). It separates both songs into vocals/bass/drums/other and predicts a per-stem chain of sox/torchaudio-style effects. That makes it a usable *group-level* hint, such as "drums need more top, other needs less low-mid", rather than a 16-channel answer. The licence of its bundled SCNet separator was not checked. automix-toolkit's `medleydb-16-dmc` checkpoint gives a free, fast gain-and-pan balance from the stems alone, which is a good sanity baseline for the fader map ([automix-toolkit](https://github.com/csteinmetz1/automix-toolkit), [dmc.py](https://github.com/csteinmetz1/automix-toolkit/blob/main/automix/models/dmc.py)). The rest do not fit the teacher role. MEGAMI (CC BY-NC-SA) and FxNorm-Automix (MIT, but pinned to torch 1.9) return **audio only**. DeepAFx-ST is single-track and research-only. ITO-Master is NC mastering. grafx-prune has no licence and needs the target mix of the same song ([MEGAMI](https://github.com/SonyResearch/MEGAMI); [FxNorm](https://github.com/sony/fxnorm-automix); [DeepAFx-ST licence](https://github.com/adobe-research/DeepAFx-ST/blob/main/LICENSE)). Consumer apps (Cryo Mix, RoEx Automix, MixingMusic AI) have no mixing API.

Claude itself is a legitimate seventh option for *starting points*. LLM2Fx showed that a general LLM, given the DSP code, measured features and five few-shot examples, predicted EQ settings far better than random or CLAP optimisation (MMD 0.22 versus 0.53 and 0.55) ([LLM2Fx](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.20770v2)). The agent study RIME found that parameter recovery collapses as instructions get vaguer, and that 95.9% of zero-shot failures came from running out of steps ([RIME](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.19605)). So Claude should get numeric targets ("vocal 2–4 kHz band 1.5 dB below F's ratio"), not taste words, and its proposals should be checked by the metric harness below.

## A concrete five-step plan to learn settings toward Mix F

**Step 1: build the render-and-measure harness (no cost).** On the build server, run a second mod-host graph identical to the live one on JACK's dummy backend in freewheel mode. Feed it 20–30 s excerpts of the 4 April stems plus one newer recording. Cache each channel's processed output, because gain, pan and sends are linear and can be summed in numpy without re-rendering. Before trusting anything, run two null tests: the same settings rendered twice must cancel, and freewheel output must match a real-time capture. The freewheel behaviour of mod-host has not been tested, so this step comes first.

**Step 2: turn Mix F and the reference into a target.** From F rendered on the 4 April stems, measure per-channel and per-group long-term spectra (1/3-octave), each channel's loudness relative to the mix (the "musical map"), crest factor and short-term LRA, and stereo width. From the Scary Pockets track, measure only the **master** spectrum, width and dynamics, never its level. The stream stays at −16 LUFS with a −2 dBTP ceiling. The objective is a weighted sum: closeness to F's map and group spectra (heavy weight, since the user chose F), plus closeness to the reference's master tone (light weight). Add hard bounds from the user's rules: drums never shrink, supporting channels stay about −4 dB, vocal stays near +2 dB, and preamp gain is never touched.

**Step 3: fit in stages.** Solve gain, pan and sends first by least squares, which takes milliseconds. Then fit EQ bands against the smoothed spectral ratio with a GRAFX/dasp proxy. Then refine EQ and compressor with CMA-ES through the real LSP/x42 plugins, using ST-ITO's settings as a start (normalised parameters, population 64, 25 steps, early stop) ([ST-ITO](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21233v1)). Keep Dragonfly presets fixed and fit only send levels. Derive the master tone with a Matchering-style curve fitted to 6–8 LSP bands. ST-ITO's 1,600 renders per fit are affordable on short excerpts, but the 2-vCPU build server will be slow. A small CPU-optimised droplet for a few hours would be cheaper than the GPU show server. **Confirm the cost before creating one.** The show-server checklist rule still blocks the GPU droplet.

**Step 4: get second opinions.** Buy the $10 RoEx pack. Send each WAV excerpt in settings-only mode with `sampleRate` 48000 and instrument groups from our channel list. Load the returned settings into the harness so they are scored on exactly the same metrics as our fit. Run StemFX on our mix against the reference for group-level hints, and DMC for a fader-map baseline. Where RoEx and our fit agree, confidence goes up. Where they disagree, that channel goes to the user's ears.

**Step 5: judge with measurements first and ears last.** Every comparison is loudness-matched (pyloudnorm, MIT; add 4× oversampled true-peak separately, since pyloudnorm lacks it ([pyloudnorm](https://github.com/csteinmetz1/pyloudnorm))). The automatic score combines multi-resolution STFT distance and audio-feature distance to the F render, the methods Diff-MST and LLM2Fx-Tools use ([LLM2Fx-Tools](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.01559v2)). Audiobox-Aesthetics "Production Quality" serves as a reference-free tiebreaker ([audiobox-aesthetics](https://github.com/facebookresearch/audiobox-aesthetics)); it has never been validated on live band mixes. AFx-Rep embedding similarity is a further option once the st-ito licence is checked. Fx-Encoder++ is CC BY-NC, so it is for private experiments only. The harness must score an identical pair as an exact tie. That one check would have exposed the Gemini failure at once. The final call is the user's, in a blind A/B page that randomises order per trial, loudness-matches both clips, and slips in duplicate pairs as catch trials. A listener, or a model, who "hears a difference" on duplicates is guessing.

### Why the audio-LLM judge must be demoted

The Gemini result was not a fluke. AudioJudge found GPT-4o-Audio and Gemini-2.5-Flash favouring one position, with the bias **growing as the two clips get closer** ([AudioJudge](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.12705)). Fine mix tweaks are exactly the near-identical case. ParaPairAudioBench found systematic position bias in every model tested, up to a 29-point gap ([ParaPairAudioBench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.24648)). CMI-RewardBench kept only order-consistent labels for that reason ([CMI-RewardBench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.00610)). Gemini stays useful for *diagnosis* ("where is the mud, which instrument is masking the vocal"). If it is ever asked to compare, it must be asked in both orders, and only consistent verdicts count. Even then it only ranks below the metrics.

## What changes when this moves to live shows

None of these systems belongs in the audio path. No neural mixer in the notes reports real-time latency, and ST-ITO-style optimisation takes about a minute per fit ([ST-ITO](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21233v1)). The live pattern that follows from the setup work is **settings snapshots**: fit or fetch settings offline, then let the deterministic controller glide to them on line-up changes, which matches the user's "unnoticeable moves only" rule. RoEx's hosted API is too slow for anything more (2–5 min per mix, 8-minute track limit). Its **C++ SDK runs offline on Linux x86_64**, but licensing is by quote, and its June 2025 "real-time" claim conflicts with the current "offline, deterministic" wording ([RoEx SDK](https://tonn-portal.roexaudio.com/sdk), [early-access blog](https://www.roexaudio.com/blog/roex-sdk-automated-multitrack-mixing-early-access)). It is worth an email only if RoEx's settings prove good in Step 4. For fast live moves, the cheaper wins are in DSP: a Faust gain-sharing automixer for the vocal mics, built from the documented Q-SYS formula ([Q-SYS](https://help.qsys.com/Content/Schematic_Library/auto_mixer.htm)), with patent US8542849 to check first ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8542849)). Beyond that, LSP sidechain ducking already extends the reverb-under-vocal pattern. One structural finding also matters: listening tests show **two-stage mixing (within groups, then between groups) beats single-stage, and bad grouping hurts most** ([Two-stage analysis](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02835)). Our channel-to-group map (drums, keys, guitars, vocals, supporting) deserves as much care as any per-channel setting.

### Claims still unverified

Several points above rest on unverified claims. Inference speeds for every model and for our own fit are unmeasured; the "seconds on GPU, minutes to hours on 2 vCPU" estimates are guesses. mod-host's behaviour under JACK freewheel is untested. x42 and Calf being LV2-only, and Dragonfly shipping VST3 builds, are from memory, not checked. RoEx quality claims are vendor-only. Its 1,000 free credits on approval conflict with a pricing page that lists none. Its enum values for `instrumentGroup` and `musicalStyle` were not captured. Whether the trained Diff-MST used its reverb module is unknown (the code has one, the paper says it lacks one). StemFX's listening numbers and the licences of SCNet and st-ito were not checked. Training-data licences behind the "Apache-2.0" DMC weights (MedleyDB) may restrict commercial use. Whether copying RoEx settings into our playbook counts as "training" under its terms is a legal question, not a technical one. One note also conflicted with another: the agent/LLM notes said MEGAMI and StemFX had no code, while direct GitHub/Hugging Face checks found public repos and checkpoints for both. This report follows the direct checks.

## Conclusion

The search changes the question from "which AI mixes best?" to "which loop learns *our* chain best?" The approved Mix F is already a parameter set, so the valuable asset is not a model but a **trustworthy render-and-measure harness**. It replays recorded shows through the exact live plugins and scores any candidate, whether from our fit, RoEx, StemFX or Claude, on the same loudness-matched metrics, with an "identical clips tie" check built in. Once that exists, each teacher costs between nothing and about a dollar per song. Because every candidate meets the same numbers, the setup phase produces auditable settings rather than opinions.

The second takeaway is that judgment is now the bottleneck, not generation. Every published system was tested on clean studio stems, and the best evidence of quality is still small listening panels with song-dependent results. For a live funk band with bleed, the user's blind A/B (with catch trials) stays the only ground truth. Metrics should narrow the field before it reaches the user, and audio-LLMs should explain problems, not pick winners.
