VIBENATION · DESIGN REPORT

Build VibeNation as a watch-and-override cockpit

Your decisions, applied throughout: on air is blue and red means errors only. Claude runs the whole show in AUTO. Engineer mode is the default view. There is no manual-mode button: any control you touch is held by you for the rest of the stream, and Claude only steps back in on it for an emergency (feedback, clipping, a dead signal). The Show screen has one picture, the live program; clicking a camera puts it on air, and Claude’s next-shot reasoning lives in the sidebar. You tap Go Live, unless a broadcast is already scheduled on YouTube. All phones broadcast in landscape, and their screens show the full camera image with nothing covering it. Output is 1080p60 and the break slate counts down from 15:00.

VibeNation should feel like StreamYard but behave like a control room run by Claude. That means one “Show” screen built around a large program monitor, a row of phone-camera tiles you can click to cut, all 16 channel faders, and a right-hand AI activity sidebar that collapses to an icon rail. A mode badge says what the AI is running. The one action a human takes by default is tapping Go Live once the AI’s pre-flight passes, unless a broadcast is already scheduled on the band’s YouTube channel, in which case the app starts it on time by itself.

This split is what the evidence supports. The tools reviewers call easiest ship a preset stage, one-click layouts and a single Go Live button rather than an empty canvas (V: 135k, 3 channels). The most-praised mixer apps show “only what this show uses” (V: 353k). Trust in automation comes from visible status, explanations, cheap undo and clear rules about who is in control (Microsoft HAX guidelines). The product also goes one step past the market: no streaming product reviewed shows why its auto-switcher cut (3 channels), so Claude’s reasoning is always readable in the sidebar.

The colour system follows your decisions: on air is blue, warnings are amber, red means errors only, and lilac marks Claude’s activity, all on VibeNation’s own “warm dark studio” surface with a single ember brand accent. The band’s name, logo and stream-graphic colours are settings, shown as “Band Name” placeholders. Every semantic colour passes WCAG contrast on the canvas (5.9:1 to 10.8:1).

Easy tools ship a stage, not a blank canvas

The strongest signal from 39 tutorial and review transcripts is that “easy” means “pre-built.” StreamYard’s studio is a stage with sources in a “backstage” tray underneath, one-click layout buttons and one Go Live button (V: 135k). Restream “walks you right into the studio” (2 channels). Mevo got a reviewer “up and running in less than five minutes,” tapping phones to switch angles live (V: 450k).

The pain points mirror this. OBS opens to a black canvas and a scene/source model the 6.3M-view beginner guide skips as too advanced (V: 6.3M). ATEM hides preview/program mode in a separate setup app (V: 337k). Mevo and StreamYard silently default to 480p and 720p (2 channels). Phone switchers take audio from the wrong device (V: 142k, vendor). Each trap becomes a rule: never show an empty canvas, never hide a show-critical mode in settings, default to the real target (1080p60), and keep one audio source of truth — the mixer.

Because the AI runs everything, the information architecture inverts the usual studio. The primary screen is a dashboard the human watches, with every control live so a touch takes it over. IBM’s “human on the loop” model fits: the AI acts autonomously while a human monitors and can override (IBM Technology; Smashing “Autonomy Dial”).

The Show screen: one live picture, click any camera to cut

Program only — Claude’s thinking lives in the sidebar

Pro switchers show preview and program side by side with CUT, AUTO and a T-bar (V: 286k; ATEM, 337k). Consumer apps skip preview and cut on tap (3 channels: Mevo, Switcher, Cinamaker). VibeNation follows the consumer pattern, by your decision: one large program monitor and a row of camera tiles. Clicking a tile puts it on air immediately, and Claude keeps directing from the shot you picked — the same override rule Ecamm uses, where a manual cut “picks up where you left off” (UNVERIFIED, 22k). The highest-engagement Dribbble studio concept uses this skeleton: a large program view with labelled controls beneath (uixNinja, 537 / 203k).

The program monitor and the live tile carry a 3 px blue border and a blue “LIVE” pill, replacing the red pill every Dribbble streaming shot uses (GoLive, 522 / 166k). Blue removes the collision where red would mean both “live” and “broken.” Musicians who expect a red tally see the word “LIVE” beside the blue.

Claude’s next-shot intention is shown in the sidebar as “Director is thinking”: “Piano solo starting — planning to cut to CAM 3 on the next downbeat.” That gives HAX feedforward (“convey the consequences before”) without putting buttons on screen. No product reviewed explains its auto-switching: Ecamm’s is “kind of a dumb switching situation” on timers, and Mevo’s Auto Director is a priority star plus a dwell slider (V: 117k, sponsored). Copy Mevo’s simple settings (priority camera, minimum/maximum dwell) into the Director’s settings.

Camera tiles are 16:9 with the phone’s name (“CAM 3 · Keys”) and corner health chips in frosted pills (Sentry, 191 / 38.8k). Hotkeys mirror pro switchers: 1–9 cut to a camera (3 channels). Add a Fade to Black that also fades audio, so there is never a hot mic after you think you are off air.

Camera onboarding: scan, choose a role, lock

The easiest join flows need no account: scan a QR code, allow permissions, pick the rear camera, start (V: 427k). Mevo Go adds a role choice, a hold-to-unlock lock so bystanders can’t change settings, and focus/exposure locks (V: 325k). Switcher lets the director set zoom, focus and exposure remotely (V: 142k, vendor).

VibeNation shows one QR code: iPhone opens the Safari camera page, Android opens the native app. The phone asks for role, name and a “plug in power” confirmation. Every phone broadcasts in landscape. The camera screen shows the full 16:9 image edge to edge with nothing on top of it, so you can frame each camera exactly as it will air; the ON AIR tally, camera name, locks and health sit in the spare strip beside the image, never over it. Framing guides are off by default. White balance and exposure are set once at pre-flight and locked for the stream; the AI cannot change them mid-show.

Health shows on the tile only when something is wrong

Geckoboard warns that “if your entire dashboard is red and green, then the significance of those colors is greatly reduced” (V: 296k). Each tile shows nothing when healthy, an amber chip (“Battery 18%”, “Wi-Fi weak”, “54 fps”) or a red chip (“Disconnected”) only on a problem. Clicking opens battery, Wi-Fi, bitrate, fps, temperature and the locked WB/exposure values. When a phone drops, the AI cuts away first and alerts second (Mevo’s “auto wide on disconnect”).

Go-live, break and end

The best observed flow is StreamYard’s: device check, a confirmation restating quality and recording, a LIVE badge with a timer, and a two-step End (V: 135k). A private test stream is the standard rehearsal (V: 414k). VibeNation’s pre-flight ticks itself off item by item: server, YouTube channel connected, scheduled broadcasts checked, 1080p60 encoder, each phone connected and charged, all 16 channels arriving with headroom, recording on, director and mixer armed. Go Live stays disabled, with the blocking item named, until everything passes.

No scheduled broadcast (the common case): you tap the big blue Go Live; the app creates the broadcast, shows “Going live in 5 · Cancel”, plays the starting-soon slate, then cuts to the opening shot. A broadcast already scheduled (rare): the app binds to it and counts down “Going live automatically in 4:12 · Hold · Go now”, starting by itself on time. Ending: in AUTO the AI announces “Ending in 0:30 · Keep live” first; a human End takes two steps.

The break slate reads “We’ll be back in…” above a 15:00 countdown in band colours. Stage mics fade down and break music fades up, so there is no hot mic. At 0:00 Claude returns to the cameras when it hears the band start. You can extend (+5 min) or end the break early.

The mixing station opens on all 16 channels

Engineers praise Mixing Station for the same layout on every console, user-set “channels per layer” and “fit channels” (V: 353k; 2 channels). The browser-based Ui24R proves a web mixer is credible: “astoundingly stable” with many clients (V: 158k; 185k).

Engineer mode is the default. All 16 inputs, buses, FX returns and the master sit on one fit-to-width layer, named and coloured by section. Tapping a strip opens a two-tier view: mini HPF, gate, EQ and compressor graphs, each opening a full editor. Double-tap resets, long-press opens typed entry (2 channels). The EQ copies FabFilter Pro-Q: drag nodes plus an overlay of competing channels’ curves (V: 127k). Dynamics get big draggable threshold lines (V: 158k). Sends-on-fader tints the background in the bus colour so you never forget you are on an in-ear mix. Ship an Outdoor high-contrast theme for daylight shows.

Simple mode (one toggle away) shows one strip per section — Drums, Bass, Keys, Guitar, Horns/Solo, Vocals — plus Room and the master with a LUFS readout against the −16 LUFS / −1 dBTP target. The Dribbble model is X-Vox: colour-coded modules and one big macro knob (Voicu Apostol, 397 / 117k).

Every AI mixer tool reviewers liked made its change visible and editable. Sonible’s smart band can be made “more extreme, less extreme, inverted” (V: 102k); Neutron users compare manual and auto snapshots (V: 162k). So each AI change is a card: plain-language sentence, the reason (often what the listener heard), before/after, an amount slider (−100% to 150%) and Undo. A muted channel leaves the AI’s calculations. Preamp gain is read-only, labelled “set at soundcheck”, and the AI never writes to the mixer.

Every AI move lands in the sidebar with a reason

Kole Jain calls the sidebar “the spine of your product”: icons with short titles that survive collapse, badges, and toasts rather than modals (V: 361k). Collapsed rails need tooltips and fixed item heights (V: 102k). The row anatomy — name, time, right-aligned status chip — comes from an AI observability design (Yuri Gokoryan, 133 / 29.3k).

The sidebar sits on the right and never moves the program monitor. Collapsed, it is a 56 px rail: the AUTO badge, one status dot per process (neutral when fine, amber warning, red error) and a white count of controls held by you. Expanded, it is a 340 px panel:

  1. Director is thinking — Claude’s current intention and reasoning, with a progress line.
  2. Running — one line per process (audio link, cameras, mix engine, listener, director, YouTube ingest), collapsing to “All systems OK” when healthy.
  3. Activity — newest first: time, agent, one-line sentence, the reason, a confidence word (“sure / likely / guessing”), and Undo or Before/after.

“Open full log” shows the raw trace, the GitHub Copilot pattern of summary in view with the session log one click away (GitHub, 75k). Bursts coalesce (“Mixer: 6 small fader moves, last 10 s”), and the panel never auto-opens during the show.

Blue means on air, red means broken, ember never touches status

The warm base is well supported on Dribbble: terracotta and copper on near-black (Erik Padamans, 301 / 68.9k), a single warm accent carrying a whole identity (Riotters, 344 / 79.7k). Conclusion

In an AI-run show the human’s job is supervision, and the evidence on supervision is about status, explanation and reversibility rather than layout. That makes the sidebar the product’s core: Claude’s stated intention, a readable diary of decisions, and undo. With Engineer mode as the default and a simple “your touch holds for the stream” rule, the full mixing depth is always there without adding a single mode to manage. Blue for on air solved a problem the research could not: red meaning both “live” and “broken.” The remaining palette risk is brand orange drifting into status use next to amber.

Three things are unproven and need rehearsal data: how readable Claude’s reasoning is at show speed, how often “emergency” overrides of held controls fire, and phone battery/thermal alerts, which have no observed precedent. Log every control you take over and every manual cut during rehearsals — each one shows where Claude’s AUTO default is not yet trusted.
